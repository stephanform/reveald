## 1. Overview

The REVEALD project draws on three complementary theoretical perspectives to
explain the adoption, quality, and consequences of mandatory sustainability
reporting under the NFRD/CSRD regulatory regime. No single theory is sufficient
to capture the full complexity of the phenomenon under study. Instead, the three
theories operate at different levels of analysis and address distinct but
interrelated aspects of the research questions:

| Theory | Level of Analysis | Core Question Addressed |
|---|---|---|
| Institutional Theory | Macro — field/sector level | Why do companies converge towards similar reporting practices? |
| Legitimacy Theory | Meso — firm level | Why does disclosure quality vary between companies? |
| Agency Theory | Micro — market level | Why does mandatory disclosure affect financial performance? |

Together they form a coherent multi-level framework that spans the regulatory
environment, firm-level reporting behaviour, and market-level consequences —
corresponding directly to the Dynamics and Tensions dimensions of the project.

## 2. Institutional Theory

### Core Argument

Institutional theory, as developed by DiMaggio and Powell (1983), explains why
organisations within the same field tend to become structurally similar over
time — a process termed **institutional isomorphism**. Rather than optimising
purely for efficiency, organisations respond to pressures from their
institutional environment, including regulatory requirements, industry norms,
and the behaviour of peer organisations.

DiMaggio and Powell (1983) identify three mechanisms driving isomorphic
convergence:

| Mechanism | Definition | Application to REVEALD |
|---|---|---|
| **Coercive isomorphism** | Formal and informal pressures exerted by organisations on which others depend, including regulatory mandates | The CSRD/ESRS framework directly coerces in-scope companies to adopt standardised sustainability reporting |
| **Mimetic isomorphism** | Imitation of successful peers under conditions of uncertainty | Companies uncertain about CSRD requirements copy disclosure practices of early adopters and industry leaders |
| **Normative isomorphism** | Diffusion of professional norms through networks of consultants, auditors, and associations | The Big Four auditing firms and sustainability consultants homogenise reporting practices across companies |

### Relevance for REVEALD

Institutional theory provides the macro-level foundation for understanding why
mandatory regulation accelerates the convergence of sustainability reporting
practices across companies, industries, and countries. It explains:

- Why the shift from voluntary (NFRD) to mandatory (CSRD) reporting produces
  measurable improvements in disclosure coverage and quality — coercive
  isomorphism raises the minimum standard
- Why disclosure quality is expected to vary systematically across industries
  with different levels of normative professionalisation
- Why cross-country variation in implementation timing (the basis of the DiD
  design) generates observable differences in reporting maturity between
  treatment and control groups

### Key Reference

> DiMaggio, P.J. and Powell, W.W. (1983). The Iron Cage Revisited:
> Institutional Isomorphism and Collective Rationality in Organizational Fields.
> *American Sociological Review*, 48(2), 147–160.
> https://doi.org/10.2307/2095101

## 3. Legitimacy Theory

### Core Argument

Legitimacy theory holds that organisations operate within a social contract
with their environment, and that their continued existence depends on
demonstrating alignment with prevailing social norms and expectations
(Suchman, 1995). Legitimacy is defined as a generalised perception that the
actions of an entity are desirable, proper, or appropriate within some socially
constructed system of norms, values, beliefs, and definitions.

Suchman (1995) distinguishes three forms of legitimacy:

| Form | Definition | Implication for Reporting |
|---|---|---|
| **Pragmatic legitimacy** | Based on the self-interest of the audience — stakeholders support the organisation because it serves their needs | Companies disclose sustainability information to satisfy investor and regulatory demands |
| **Moral legitimacy** | Based on normative approval — the organisation is seen as doing the right thing | Companies with strong sustainability performance use disclosure to signal genuine commitment |
| **Cognitive legitimacy** | Based on taken-for-grantedness — the organisation conforms to widely shared assumptions | Sustainability reporting becomes a standard expectation; non-disclosure signals deviance |

### Symbolic vs. Substantive Reporting

A critical distinction for REVEALD is between **symbolic** and **substantive**
legitimacy-seeking behaviour:

- **Symbolic reporting** — companies produce disclosures that appear compliant
  on the surface but lack genuine informational content (boilerplate language,
  vague targets, absence of metrics). This is consistent with low DQS scores
  despite technical compliance.
- **Substantive reporting** — companies make genuine changes to governance,
  risk management, and performance measurement that are then reflected in
  high-quality, granular disclosures. This is consistent with high DQS scores
  and measurable improvements in sustainability indicators.

The distinction between symbolic and substantive reporting is operationalised
in the Disclosure Quality Scoring Model (DQS) and is central to the
**disclosure-performance gap** robustness check in WP6.

### Relevance for REVEALD

Legitimacy theory explains firm-level variation in disclosure quality that
institutional theory — which predicts convergence — cannot fully account for:

- Companies facing stronger stakeholder scrutiny (large firms, consumer-facing
  industries, high analyst coverage) are expected to produce higher-quality
  disclosures to maintain pragmatic and moral legitimacy
- The transition from NFRD to CSRD raises the cognitive legitimacy threshold —
  what was acceptable under NFRD may no longer satisfy stakeholder expectations
  under CSRD
- The Tensions dimension of REVEALD — the interaction between sustainability
  reporting and financial performance — can be partially explained by the cost
  of legitimacy maintenance: companies investing in substantive reporting incur
  short-term costs but may gain long-term legitimacy benefits

### Key Reference

> Suchman, M.C. (1995). Managing Legitimacy: Strategic and Institutional
> Approaches. *Academy of Management Review*, 20(3), 571–610.
> https://doi.org/10.5465/amr.1995.9508080331

## 4. Agency Theory

### Core Argument

Agency theory, as formalised by Jensen and Meckling (1976), analyses the
relationship between a **principal** (e.g. shareholders, investors) and an
**agent** (e.g. company management) in which the agent is hired to act on
behalf of the principal. This relationship generates **agency costs** arising
from three sources:

| Cost Type | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Monitoring costs** | Costs incurred by the principal to observe and constrain agent behaviour |
| **Bonding costs** | Costs incurred by the agent to signal trustworthiness to the principal |
| **Residual loss** | Value loss resulting from divergence between agent decisions and principal interests |

The central problem is **information asymmetry** — managers possess more
information about the true financial and sustainability performance of the
company than external stakeholders. This asymmetry creates incentives for
opportunistic behaviour, including the strategic management of disclosures.

### Mandatory Disclosure as an Agency Cost Reduction Mechanism

Mandatory sustainability reporting under the CSRD can be understood as a
regulatory mechanism to reduce agency costs by:

- **Forcing information production** — companies must generate and disclose
  sustainability data they might otherwise withhold
- **Standardising disclosure** — ESRS comparability reduces the ability of
  managers to present selectively favourable information
- **Requiring assurance** — third-party verification (a dimension of the DQS)
  reduces the credibility gap between disclosed and actual performance

When agency costs are reduced through improved disclosure, capital markets
respond with lower information risk premiums, translating into lower cost of
capital and potentially higher firm valuation — the mechanism underpinning
Hypothesis H4.

### Relevance for REVEALD

Agency theory provides the micro-level, market-oriented foundation for the
Tensions dimension of the project:

- Higher DQS scores reduce information asymmetry between management and
  investors, which is expected to lower the cost of capital and improve
  financial performance metrics over time
- The moderating role of analyst coverage (a control variable in WP6) is
  directly grounded in agency theory — analysts function as external monitors
  whose presence reduces information asymmetry independently of mandatory
  disclosure
- The non-linear relationship between disclosure quality and financial
  performance (H4) reflects the trade-off between short-term compliance costs
  (bonding costs in Jensen & Meckling's terminology) and long-term agency cost
  reduction benefits

### Key Reference

> Jensen, M.C. and Meckling, W.H. (1976). Theory of the Firm: Managerial
> Behavior, Agency Costs and Ownership Structure. *Journal of Financial
> Economics*, 3(4), 305–360.
> https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-405X(76)90026-X

## 5. Integration: A Three-Level Framework

The three theories are not competing explanations but complementary lenses
operating at different levels of analysis. Together they form a coherent
multi-level theoretical framework for REVEALD:

```
MACRO LEVEL — Institutional Theory
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Regulatory field (EU):  NFRD → CSRD/ESRS
Isomorphic pressures:   Coercive (regulation)
                        Mimetic (peer imitation)
                        Normative (auditors, consultants)
Effect:                 Convergence of reporting practices
                        across companies and countries
        ↓
MESO LEVEL — Legitimacy Theory
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Firm behaviour:         Symbolic vs. substantive reporting
Legitimacy forms:       Pragmatic (investor demands)
                        Moral (sustainability commitment)
                        Cognitive (taken-for-grantedness)
Effect:                 Variation in disclosure quality (DQS)
                        within the regulated population
        ↓
MICRO LEVEL — Agency Theory
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Market mechanism:       Information asymmetry reduction
Agency cost channels:   Monitoring, bonding, residual loss
Effect:                 Lower cost of capital, improved
                        financial performance (ROA/ROE)
                        — the Tensions dimension
```

This cascade structure means that institutional forces set the regulatory
context, legitimacy dynamics determine how firms respond within that context,
and agency theory explains the market-level consequences of variation in
response quality.

## 6. Mapping Theory to Research Questions and Hypotheses

| RQ / Hypothesis | Primary Theory | Secondary Theory |
|---|---|---|
| RQ1: Has CSRD improved reporting quality? | Institutional (coercive isomorphism) | Legitimacy (symbolic vs. substantive) |
| RQ2: How does disclosure interact with financial performance? | Agency (information asymmetry) | Legitimacy (legitimacy costs) |
| RQ3: How does quality vary across countries and industries? | Institutional (normative isomorphism) | Legitimacy (stakeholder pressure) |
| H1: CSRD companies show higher DQS | Institutional | — |
| H2: Effect moderated by size, industry, country | Institutional + Legitimacy | — |
| H3: Higher DQS → improved sustainability indicators | Legitimacy (substantive reporting) | Agency (credible commitment) |
| H4: Non-linear DQS → financial performance | Agency | Legitimacy (compliance costs) |
